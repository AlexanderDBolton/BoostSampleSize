Rerandomization

rerandomization
design
inference

Randomising once can hand you arms that differ on the very things you were trying to hold constant. Rerandomization refuses those splits before the experiment starts.

Published

August 24, 2026

You randomise a few hundred people into two arms, and then you look at the baseline table. The treatment group is a year older. It is also, because age and blood pressure travel together, slightly hypertensive. Nothing has gone wrong — this is what randomisation does, and it will happen to roughly one experiment in twenty for any covariate you care to check. But you now get to spend the rest of the study explaining it, and the imbalance sits in your standard errors whether you explain it or not.

Rerandomization takes the obvious way out. Randomisation is cheap, so draw a split, measure how unbalanced it is, and if it is unacceptable, throw it away and draw another. Keep going until you get one you would be happy to defend. The result is still a genuinely random assignment — you simply drew it from a smaller pool of good ones. All of the work happens before a single unit is treated, and the only cost is CPU time.

How it works

The measure of imbalance is the Mahalanobis distance from the previous article, which is what lets you collapse a whole covariate table into one number without double-counting the covariates that say the same thing.

Concretely, with \(N\) units, \(k\) covariates and two equal arms:

  1. Compute the covariate covariance matrix \(S\) once, from all \(N\) units.
  2. Draw a candidate split. Take the two arms’ covariate mean vectors, \(\bar X_T\) and \(\bar X_C\).
  3. The imbalance score is the squared Mahalanobis distance between those two mean vectors, scaled by \(N/4\): \[ M \;=\; \frac{N}{4}\,(\bar X_T - \bar X_C)^{\!\top} S^{-1} (\bar X_T - \bar X_C). \]
  4. If \(M \le a\), keep the split. Otherwise discard it and go back to step 2.

Step 1 is the one worth flagging: \(S\) describes the pool of units, not the split, so it never changes as you redraw. Compute it once, outside the loop, along with whatever factorisation you use to apply \(S^{-1}\). Every later iteration is then just a pair of means and a quadratic form.

The \(N/4\) is not decoration. Under complete randomisation the mean-difference vector has covariance \(\tfrac{4}{N} S\), so that factor is exactly what turns \(M\) into the squared Mahalanobis distance of the imbalance from its own null distribution — which is what makes the next section possible.

Choosing the threshold

Because \(M\) is a squared Mahalanobis distance of an approximately normal \(k\)-vector, it follows a chi-square distribution on \(k\) degrees of freedom. So the threshold is a chi-square quantile. To accept a proportion \(p_a\) of draws,

\[ a = F^{-1}_{\chi^2_k}(p_a), \qquad \text{accept if } M \le a . \]

The expected number of draws before one is accepted is \(1/p_a\). That is the entire cost model: a 1-in-1,000 threshold costs about 1,000 draws.

A budget instead of a threshold

Picking \(p_a\) means committing to an unbounded loop: you will probably accept within a few thousand draws, but nothing guarantees it. The alternative is to fix a budget — draw 10,000 splits, keep the single best one — which runs in known time and needs no threshold at all.

Morgan and Rubin recommend exactly this, in §5.2 of their 2015 tiers paper:

In practice we expect to see little difference between rerandomizing until an allocation with \(M \le a\) is obtained, and choosing the allocation with the smallest \(M\) from \(1/p_a\) randomizations, where \(p_a = P(M \le a)\).

That is offered as an expectation rather than a result — the paper gives no proof or simulation for it — so it is worth checking. The two schemes are genuinely different: the winner of a tournament of \(B\) draws is not guaranteed to clear the 1-in-\(B\) bar, and misses it about \((1 - 1/B)^B \approx 37\%\) of the time. Best-of-\(B\) is therefore always the weaker of the two.

The ratio column looks alarming at \(k = 2\) — twice the imbalance variance — but read the absolute numbers next to it: \(0.00005\) against \(0.00010\). Both schemes have annihilated the imbalance, and doubling almost nothing leaves almost nothing. The place where the difference could matter is at large \(k\), where a meaningful fraction of the imbalance survives, and that is precisely where the two schemes converge: 6% apart at twenty covariates, 3% at fifty.

So Morgan and Rubin’s expectation holds up, for a reason their sentence does not give: the gap is largest exactly where both schemes are already good enough for it not to matter.

Pick a budget you can afford and keep the best draw. It removes the one genuinely awkward parameter and it runs in known time. Just record that this is what you did, because the inference has to match the scheme you actually ran.

The acceptance region

With two covariates you can see the thing. Each dot below is one candidate split, placed at its pair of covariate mean differences. The cloud is tilted because height and weight are correlated: a split that over-represents tall people over-represents heavy ones too.

The acceptance region is the ellipse. At a 5% acceptance rate it is so much smaller than the cloud that it is barely a smudge at the origin, which is the honest visual point — so drag to zoom in on it.

Zoomed all the way in, the green dots are the splits you would have kept. Notice that they are not the ones where both imbalances are near zero — they are the ones inside a tilted ellipse, which tolerates a larger height imbalance when weight is imbalanced the same way, and is strict about the two disagreeing. That tilt is \(\Sigma^{-1}\) doing its job, and it is the reason the criterion is a Mahalanobis distance rather than a sum of squared z-scores.

How much does it buy you?

Fix one covariate — say age — and ask how much its arm-to-arm difference bounces around from experiment to experiment.

  • \(\operatorname{Var}_{\text{CR}}\) is that variance under complete randomisation: draw once, accept whatever you get.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}_{\text{RR}}\) is the same variance under rerandomization: draw repeatedly, keep only splits with \(M \le a\).

Rerandomization can only shrink it, and Morgan and Rubin showed it shrinks by the same factor for every covariate at once:

\[ v_a \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\text{RR}}}{\operatorname{Var}_{\text{CR}}} \;=\; \frac{P(\chi^2_{k+2} \le a)}{P(\chi^2_{k} \le a)} . \]

Read \(v_a\) as “the fraction of the imbalance that survives”. \(v_a = 1\) means rerandomization bought nothing; \(v_a = 0.05\) means 95% of the imbalance variance is gone.

The one number worth memorising is what a 1-in-10,000 threshold delivers as the covariate count grows.

Five covariates at 1-in-10,000 leaves about 1% of the imbalance. Fifty leaves 40%. The budget does not scale: every covariate you add has to be balanced simultaneously, and the acceptance region shrinks in every direction at once. Do not throw covariates at the criterion because you have them — each one you add dilutes the balance achieved on the ones you actually care about.

ImportantBalance is not the goal; precision is

The variance of your treatment effect estimate falls by a factor of \(1 - (1 - v_a)R^2\), where \(R^2\) is the squared multiple correlation between the outcome and the covariates you balanced.

That \(R^2\) term is doing at least as much work as \(v_a\). If the covariates do not predict the outcome, perfect balance on them buys you exactly nothing. Choosing which covariates to balance matters more than choosing how hard to balance them — and it is the decision people spend the least time on.

Inference after rerandomization

Outline — to be written.

Rerandomization changes the design, so it changes what “by chance” means. If you rerandomize and then run an ordinary \(t\)-test on the difference in means, the test is conservative: it prices in imbalance the design already removed, so your intervals are too wide and you give back the precision you just paid for.

Li, Ding and Rubin (2018) derive what the distribution actually is. The difference in means is no longer asymptotically normal — it is a normal plus a truncated-normal term, the truncation coming from the acceptance rule. Its variance is a factor \(1 - (1 - v_a)R^2\) smaller than the \(t\)-test assumes, where \(R^2\) is the squared multiple correlation between the outcome and the balanced covariates.

How much is that worth?

Standard errors shrink by \(\sqrt{1 - (1 - v_a)R^2}\), so the whole gain is governed by \(R^2\) — how well the covariates predict the outcome — and barely at all by how hard you rerandomized.

With covariates that explain 20% of the outcome, the correction is worth about a tenth of your interval width — real, but unlikely to change a conclusion. At \(R^2 = 0.8\) it halves the interval, which certainly would. So the question is not “did I rerandomize hard enough” but “do my covariates predict the outcome at all”.

Do you have to derive it for your own estimator?

Mostly, no — and this is the part worth knowing before you reach for the closed form. Three routes, in increasing order of effort:

  1. Adjust for the balancing covariates in your regression. Li and Ding
    1. show that once the analysis adjusts for the variables used in the design, asymptotic normality comes back and conventional standard errors are justified again. The truncated term was the part of the imbalance the covariates explain, so regressing on them absorbs it. This is what most practitioners already do by instinct, it costs nothing, and it is the default I would recommend.
  2. Randomisation inference. Re-draw assignments under the same acceptance rule, recompute your estimator each time, and read the reference distribution off the results. This needs no new theory and works for any estimator — difference-in-differences, clustered errors, instrumental variables — which makes it the general answer. The cost is compute: your whole estimation routine runs thousands of times. It is also the only clean option if you used a best-of-\(B\) budget, since the acceptance rule is then not a simple threshold.
  3. A closed-form correction for your estimator. The Li–Ding–Rubin result is for the difference in means. For a difference-in-differences regression you would need the corresponding theory; recent work extends this to general M-estimators, but deriving it yourself for a bespoke specification is a research project, not an analysis step.

Planned content: a worked randomisation test against the same acceptance rule, and a simulation checking how much the adjusted-regression route gives up against the exact correction.

WarningUse the covariate values as they stood at the moment of splitting

This is the question I could not find answered cleanly anywhere, so to state it plainly: the analysis must use the pre-randomisation values of the balancing covariates, not their latest values.

Suppose you ran a development experiment across villages and balanced on village population. By the end of the study, populations have changed — and if the intervention worked, it may well have caused some of that change, through migration or falling child mortality. Adjusting for end-of-study population would then be conditioning on something the treatment affected. That is post-treatment bias: it removes part of the very effect you are trying to measure, and it can flip the sign of an estimate.

The design argument points the same way. The randomisation distribution is defined by the acceptance rule, and that rule was evaluated against the baseline numbers. Those are the values that determine which splits could have occurred, so those are the values the analysis has to condition on. Freeze your covariate table when you draw the split, and keep it.

Covariate adjustment as an alternative

Outline — to be written.

If you are going to regress the outcome on the covariates anyway, why bother balancing them in the design? Planned content:

  • What adjustment alone gets you. Regression can remove imbalance after the fact, and for a linear outcome model it recovers most of what rerandomization would have delivered.
  • Why the design stage still earns its keep. Adjustment is a modelling choice made after you have seen the data, which invites specification search; balance is a property of the assignment, fixed before any outcome exists. Severe imbalance also makes adjustment an extrapolation rather than an interpolation.
  • The honest comparison. A simulation over linear outcomes, non-linear outcomes, and a case where the covariates are irrelevant — which is where rerandomization has the least to offer and adjustment quietly costs degrees of freedom.

Practical notes and failure modes

Feasibility at small \(N\). There are only \(\binom{N}{N/2}\) distinct equal splits. That is astronomically large for \(N = 200\), but for \(N = 20\) it is 184,756, and an acceptance rate of \(10^{-6}\) is then not merely slow but impossible. Check that \(p_a \binom{N}{N/2} \gg 1\).

More covariates is not free. A correlated covariate gets no extra weight in \(M\), but it does raise \(k\), which shrinks the benefit available at any budget.

Every covariate is treated as equally important. \(M\) is invariant to rescaling, which is a virtue for units and a limitation for priorities. If some covariates genuinely matter more, use tiered rerandomization (Morgan and Rubin, 2015), which applies separate thresholds to groups of covariates in order of importance.

Keep the accepted split reproducible. Record the seed, the covariate table, the encoding and the threshold. \(S\) is computed from the data at hand, so changing the covariate set changes the metric and therefore the accepted split.
